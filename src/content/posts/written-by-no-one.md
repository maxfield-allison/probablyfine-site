A post came across my feed the other day explaining LLMs, RAG, MCP, and agents.

One sentence per line.

Like this.

"Here's the simplest way to think about all four." Then the big finish: these technologies
don't compete, they build on each other. Wow. Thanks.

Nobody wrote that post. Someone prompted it, skimmed it, and hit publish. And it did numbers,
which is the part that actually bugs me.

I get why. The format works, the algorithm eats it up, and if your goal is "post consistently
to build my personal brand," then a machine that produces infinite agreeable content in the
house style of LinkedIn is basically free money. I'm not even sure I'd behave differently if my
impressions were the thing paying my rent.

But it's costing the rest of us. I now read every post with skepticism about whether a human
thought it. That suspicion ends up landing on the guy who spent his
Saturday morning writing up a real production incident, because his post is sitting in a feed
where so much of everything has that same smooth, frictionless, nothing-to-disagree-with
texture. He pays the tax too. His real thing gets scrolled past for looking like the fakes.

You can feel the texture once you notice it. No specifics. No stakes. No sentence where the
author risked anything. It reads like it was optimized to offend no one and inform no one,
because it was. That's literally what the model is doing: averaging over everyone who ever
wrote about the topic, and the average of everyone has no opinions.

And that's just the lazy version. On top of it, people are writing prompts in sequenced
frameworks that specifically target the ways tens or hundreds of different kinds of people
would read and critique a post. What comes out of that is completely optimized for engagement,
the currency of the internet as we know it today.

I don't think most of it is even that deliberate. The majority isn't optimizing for anything. Having nothing to say is certainly not a new condition, and neither is posting anyway. The model didn't invent the emptiness. It amplified a laziness that was already there and made it free to publish at
volume. (I even cringe at writing out an "It isn't X. It's Y" now.)

And the loop runs both ways now. There's so much of this "content" in circulation that even
our natural writing is starting to take cues from the slop.

The annoying part is how cheap the fix is. One thing that actually happened to you. One opinion
someone could argue with. One "I don't know." Any little human mistake or error or unpolished sentence. That's it. That's the whole difference between a
person and a plausible-sounding nobody.

## Then I posted it

A version of the above went out as a LinkedIn post a few weeks ago. (It was a bit hyperbolic and also claimed 60
percent of the feed has this texture. I pulled that number out of thin air, which is exactly the
kind of thing this post is against. It's "a lot" now.)

The first comment came from a guy I've known since middle school: "This feels like AI
to me lol! I've gotten to the point where EVERYTHING has that same generated cadence and/or
tone."

He meant it as agreement. At least I think he did. I can't tell you for certain whether "this"
meant the posts I was describing or the one I had just written, and I'd bet he couldn't cleanly
separate it either. Somebody who has known me since before either of us could drive now reads
my writing with part of his brain asking whether I wrote it. I don't think he doubts me. Doubting is
just the default setting now.

I replied that everyone and everything is now suspect. He agreed and made a joke about staring
suspiciously at his toaster.

I wrote a whole post about the suspicion tax and the first person through the door demonstrated
it on me. I couldn't stage a cleaner proof if I tried.

## The numbers, since I brought up numbers

The slop post that set me off did numbers. Mine did 240 impressions and six reactions.

I'm not complaining. 240 is about what an opinion post from a guy with my reach gets, and I
didn't write it for the algorithm. But look at the mechanics with me: the post optimized to
offend no one, full of information anyone could "ChatGPT" for themselves, outperforms the post
a person meant, in the same feed, competing for the same attention. The ranking function prefers the average, because the average is safe to show
anyone. So the fakes that create the suspicion tax outrank the real posts anyway.

## How is this post any different

There's a tag at the top of this one that says AI was involved, and I owe you the difference
between this and what I've been complaining about.

The slop workflow is "write me a post about LLMs, RAG, MCP, and agents," a skim, and the
publish button. The human contribution is a topic and a thumb. Nothing in the result happened
to the author. No sentence costs them anything. Swap in a different author and the post comes
out the same.

This one went the other way around. The opinion existed first, in public, under my name. The
expansion started from an outline I argued over before any prose existed, the draft was written
against a pile of my own writing samples and a rulebook built from my own edits, and I struck
the sentences I wouldn't say. The machine typed faster than I would have. It didn't get annoyed
three weeks ago, it doesn't know the guy from middle school, and it had no opinion about any of
this until I gave it mine.

And yes, I hear it: that's a sequenced framework too. So the difference can't be the framework.
It's what the thing is optimized for. The engagement pipeline tunes a post toward whatever the
most readers will react to. Mine tunes a draft toward what I would have said anyway, and throws
out everything I wouldn't. Most of the text is changed, addendums are made, edits go back and
forth to make sure I'm not undermining my own point or making silly literary mistakes.

I use AI for writing all the time, so none of this is a purity thing. "Did AI touch the text"
turns out to be the wrong question. Ask instead what in the text would survive "how do you know
that?" A real answer sounds like: it happened to me, here's the comment, here's the number,
here's the friend. Slop dies on that question, and it dies fast, because there was never
anybody home.

If your post could have been written by anyone, it was effectively written by no one.

Anyway. If you've read this far, congrats. You've found one of the posts a human being means.
My friend will have to take my word for it.

It's probably fine.
