I wasn't trying to find out what the homelab cost. I was trying to find out when things happened.

There's no git history for the first nine years of this hobby. The oldest commit in any of my infrastructure repos is from November 2025, and the machines it describes had already been through four rebuilds by then. So when I wanted to write about how the thing evolved, I had to go looking for something that remembered better than I did. Purchase history turned out to be the only continuous record: 1,860 Amazon orders going back to 2012, every one of them timestamped, most of them describing exactly which part went into which box.

It worked. I got my dates. I also got a total, which I had not asked for.

## The number

Filtering that export down to anything that could plausibly be called homelab or computing gives **456 orders and $38,743.69**, spread across fourteen years. That's about $2,767 a year.

I want to be careful with that figure, because I've already said a different one out loud. When this came up on Facebook a few weeks ago I said $33,708, and I wasn't being sloppy. The gap is entirely inclusion rules. Count the cables, adapters, monitors and KVMs and you land near $38,700. Drop them and it's $30,971. Drop the cameras and smart plugs too and you're at $27,500. Every one of those numbers is defensible depending on what you think a homelab is, and $33,708 sits comfortably in the middle of them.

So take the range, not the point. And take all of it as a floor, because the export only knows about Amazon. It has nothing from eBay, where I bought most of the used enterprise gear. Nothing from the drives I picked up in stores. Nothing gifted. The switch that runs my whole network cost about $150 on eBay and does not appear in that $38,743 anywhere.

The biggest single year was 2022 at $10,719, which was the year I built the rack.

## The question I got asked

I posted the total on Facebook mostly as a joke about my own bad decisions, and the responses were more interesting than the post.

My wife's, immediately: "Only $2300 a year lol." Then, a minute later: "Still cheaper than the 'zoo'." We have a lot of animals. She's right on both counts, and dividing by fourteen does more to make a five-figure number reasonable than any argument I could construct.

My mother's was the one that stuck, though. She asked whether it earned me anything, whether it moved me up at work, and suggested it might have paid for itself.

I said yes, and I meant it. Nearly everything I do professionally now, I learned first on hardware I bought with my own money to solve a problem nobody was paying me to solve. That's a real return and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

But I've been sitting with the question for a few weeks and I don't think it's the right one. Not because the answer is bad. Because the thing it's measuring is the part that was never expensive.

## The other hobby

While I was in the receipts I pulled my Steam history too, on the theory that if I was going to be honest about one hobby I might as well be honest about the adjacent one.

Steam has taken **$13,072 across 439 purchases** since 2014. Call it a thousand a year, a third of what the homelab costs, and nobody has ever asked me to justify a cent of it.

It also told me something the Amazon export can't. Steam counts hours. Across 288 games with playtime recorded, it has logged **15,399 of them**.

That's about a year and nine months. Continuous. Not "since I was a kid," either, because Steam only knows about Steam. The console years before it have no ledger at all.

## What doesn't have a number

Here's the part that turned this from a spreadsheet into a post.

I know, to one decimal place, how long I have spent playing video games. Steam tracked it without being asked, in the background, for twelve years, and will show it to me on a page sorted by hours descending.

I have no idea how long I've spent on the homelab. Nothing counted it. There's no session timer on racking a server, no achievement for the third time you rebuild a cluster because you learned something that invalidates the second time. The receipts record the exact moment money left my account and say nothing whatsoever about the evening that followed it, which is where the actual cost was.

And money is the easy number, which is precisely why it's the one that gets argued about. It's legible. It totals. You can put it in a Facebook post and your mother can reasonably ask whether it paid for itself, because $38,743 is a thing with an answer.

The hours don't total. I could not tell you within a factor of two.

## Where I caught myself

When I started adding up the non-Amazon vendors, I was doing it because the number felt incomplete. eBay, Newegg, B&H, the Google Workspace subscription I ran for years and have been trying to get off of because it costs more per month than I want to admit in print. All of that is real spending that belongs in an honest total, and I went looking for it with some enthusiasm.

Then I noticed what I was doing. Every receipt I was hunting for was also a night I didn't account for. The eBay orders came with evenings. The Workspace bill came with a migration. Widening the search was going to make the money number bigger and the time number no clearer at all, and I was going to end up with a more precise answer to the question that mattered less.

So I stopped, mostly. The vendor totals will get added eventually because I'm constitutionally incapable of leaving a dataset half-mined. But the post isn't waiting on them, because they can't change the shape of it.

## So

$38,743.69, plus an unknown amount from eBay, over fourteen years, to learn a career on. That's the cheap part and I'd do it again without thinking about it very hard.

The expensive part isn't in any export I own. It was Tuesday nights. It was the rebuild that didn't need to happen. It was every hour I spent on a rack in a garage that Steam would have logged for me if I'd been shooting at something instead.

I'm not going to start tracking it. Some numbers are worse when you know them.
